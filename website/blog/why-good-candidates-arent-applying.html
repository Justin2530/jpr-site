Why the Right Candidates Aren't Applying to Your Job Opening

October 7, 2026 · JPR

You put the opening out there, wait a week or two, and the applications trickle in. A few look close. Most don't. The person you actually pictured for the role never shows up.

That's not bad luck. It's how hiring works for most skilled positions, especially in a smaller labor market like Jefferson County and the counties around it.

The best people usually aren't looking

Think about the machinist, maintenance tech or office manager you'd hire tomorrow if you could. Odds are they already have a job. They show up, they're good at what they do, and their current employer wants to keep them.

People like that don't spend their evenings scrolling through openings. They aren't unhappy enough to go searching, but many of them would listen if the right opportunity came to them directly. That's the group a posted opening rarely reaches.

Who you hear from instead

When you wait for applications, you mostly hear from people who are actively looking. Some of them are great. But that group also includes people who are between jobs for a reason, people applying to everything they see, and people whose background only loosely matches what you need.

Sorting through that takes time, and time is usually the thing you're short on when a position is open.

What it costs to wait

An open position costs more than the time spent reviewing applications. Someone else is covering that work, overtime adds up, projects slip and the people picking up the slack get worn down. The longer the role sits open, the more pressure there is to settle for "close enough."

What works better

The employers who fill skilled positions consistently don't just wait. They go find people:

  • Identify people whose background fits. Start from the skills and experience the job actually needs, then look for people who already have them, whether or not they're job hunting.
  • Reach out directly. A personal message about a specific opportunity gets a very different response than a listing someone might scroll past.
  • Lead with what matters to them. Pay, schedule, commute and stability usually matter more than a list of requirements. Be clear about those early.
  • Screen before anyone's time is spent. A short conversation up front confirms interest, pay expectations and availability, so interviews are only with people who make sense.
  • Move quickly. Good candidates who are approached directly still have other options, and the process should respect that.
